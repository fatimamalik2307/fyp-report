\chapter{Literature Review / Related Work}

Clinical decision support draws on medical language models, imaging analysis, evidence retrieval and agent coordination. The following review examines related applications, methods and resources, comparing their capabilities and limitations to identify the research gap.

\section{Detailed Literature Review}

This section reviews systems, models, datasets and methods related to Zeest. The topic is divided into clinical decision support, medical language models, imaging and multimodal learning, retrieval-based evidence support, agentic orchestration and clinical data resources. The purpose of this review is to identify what work already supports and where Zeest remains different. Thus, the literature is discussed according to the capability it contributes to the physician-controlled, longitudinal and multimodal clinical workflow. The comparison distinguishes clinical applications from representation models, specialist classifiers, retrieval methods and datasets. Each source is assessed for the task it demonstrates and the limits of transferring that capability into an encounter workflow. This distinction prevents medical question-answering performance or image classification findings from being presented as evidence of a clinical system. Source references identify the reviewed work, while critical assessment examines the additional ownership, provenance and review controls required by Zeest. Comparison distinguishes published capability from integration.

\subsection{Clinical Decision Support and Electronic Records}
\subsubsection{Research Overview}

Isabel DDx represents a differential diagnosis support application that helps clinicians consider possible diagnoses from entered features.~\cite{zeestB:isabel} Its strength is that it addresses a real clinical reasoning task and can broaden the physician's diagnostic consideration. Its limitation for Zeest is that it is not described as a longitudinal patient-memory system with multimodal agents, audit-controlled approval and an ablation framework. UpToDate provides evidence-based clinical reference material for clinicians and is useful as a point of care knowledge resource.~\cite{zeestB:uptodate} Its reviewed reference capability motivates evidence access, while Zeest separately defines patient memory, specialist processing and revision-specific approval. OpenMRS demonstrates how an open source EMR can support patient record management, especially in resource-constrained settings.~\cite{zeestB:openmrs} Its relevance is strongest for patient and encounter organisation, while its limitation is that it is a record platform rather than an agentic reasoning and evidence-review workflow. Published capability does not establish completed integration or clinical effectiveness within Zeest.

\subsubsection{Critical Assessment}

The reviewed systems are strong in their individual areas. Isabel DDx supports differential diagnosis exploration, UpToDate gives clinicians access to curated medical reference content and OpenMRS shows how patient records can be organised in an open source EMR. Their documented contributions concern distinct parts of the target workflow. Differential diagnosis support does not automatically preserve encounter-specific memory, a reference system does not manage patient and encounter context, and an EMR does not by itself generate evidence-grounded drafts through specialist agents. Zeest additionally specifies revision-specific approval, modification, rejection and audit handling. Their limitations show why Zeest needs a combined method rather than one existing clinical tool. A useful assessment must distinguish the capability demonstrated by the source from the additional workflow Zeest requires. Integration still needs patient ownership, identifiable source relationships and explicit review states. These responsibilities cannot be inferred from benchmark performance or the presence of an existing software tool.

\subsubsection{Relevance to Zeest}

These systems show that Zeest should not treat clinical decision support as only a text-generation task. Differential diagnosis applications, medical references and EMR platforms each supply useful ideas for the project: possible diagnosis review, cited clinical knowledge and structured patient records. However, the integration problem concerns the connection between these ideas. A physician needs to view the current consultation, retrieve relevant longitudinal history, inspect specialist findings and approve or reject the resulting draft. Existing reference or EMR systems do not automatically answer how a generated recommendation should be linked to a specific encounter, supporting evidence and audit action. Chapter~4 therefore specifies patient selection, encounter history, consultation note correction, evidence inspection and revision-specific approval as separate behaviours. The literature supports those boundaries by showing that information access and decision support are different responsibilities. Zeest's contribution is the integration of those responsibilities under physician control, not replacement of existing clinical judgement.

\subsection{Medical and Biomedical Language Models}
\subsubsection{Research Overview}

Med-PaLM 2 demonstrates that large language models can achieve strong performance on medical question answering benchmarks after medical alignment and evaluation.~\cite{zeestB:medpalm2} GPT-4 medical challenge work shows broad medical reasoning capability across examination-style tasks, while documenting safety and reliability concerns that matter in clinical use.~\cite{zeestA:noriMedical} These studies motivate the use of a powerful language model within Zeest, but their task setting is different from a longitudinal clinical workflow. Benchmark questions do not require storing a patient's encounter history, associating outputs with a physician action or preserving the difference between a draft and an approved clinical decision. The literature supports cautious use of language models as reasoning components. It does not support autonomous diagnosis, unreviewed treatment or the assumption that benchmark performance proves patient-level reliability. Zeest addresses this limitation by requiring retrieved evidence, critic review and explicit physician approval before any generated content becomes a recorded clinical decision. Component selection remains unresolved.

\subsubsection{Critical Assessment}

ClinicalBERT and BioGPT show another important direction: adapting language models to biomedical and text. ClinicalBERT provides publicly contextual embeddings trained on clinical notes, thereby contributing to patient-record representations and clinical NLP tasks.~\cite{zeestB:clinicalbert} BioGPT applies generative pre-training to biomedical text generation and mining, showing that domain-specific corpora can improve biomedical language processing.~\cite{zeestB:biogpt} Their strength is representation of specialised language, terminology and document patterns. Their limitation for Zeest is that they do not by themselves provide a physician workflow, multimodal processing path or approval-controlled memory update. They do not solve the problem of grounding a draft in patient-specific evidence. For Zeest, these works support the general choice to use medical-language capability and semantic representations, but the embedding model and LLM remain unresolved. The methodology must treat model selection as a dependency and avoid claiming that any biomedical model has already been integrated. The task and integration boundary must remain distinguishable during evaluation.

\subsubsection{Relevance to Zeest}

These language-model studies are related to Zeest because the proposed system requires clinical text understanding, structured note use, reasoning over patient case context and generation of a reviewable draft. Their results support the decision to include language-model capability, but they also explain why Zeest requires additional controls. The proposed work adds longitudinal patient memory, cited evidence retrieval, critic physician review and physician approval around the generative component. In this way, Zeest uses medical language modelling as one part of a controlled clinical workflow rather than treating a model answer as an accepted clinical decision. The connection also creates verification targets: case inputs retain their originating sources, processing failures remain visible, and draft revisions preserve the evidence and critic findings presented to the responsible physician. Component value must be compared under common case definitions and reference procedures. Resource selection does not change the requirement for explicit approval before clinical decision recording.

\subsection{Medical Imaging and Multimodal Learning}
\subsubsection{Research Overview}

CheXNet used deep learning for pneumonia detection on chest radiographs and is frequently cited as evidence that imaging models can reach strong performance on a narrow radiology task.~\cite{zeestB:chexnet} CheXpert then provided a large chest radiograph dataset with uncertainty labels and an expert comparison benchmark across fourteen observations.~\cite{zeestB:chexpert} These works are because Zeest intends to integrate a validated imaging classification model rather than train a new diagnostic model from scratch. Their limitation is equally important: a chest X-ray classifier produces findings for an imaging task, not a decision. It does not know the patient's longitudinal history, consultation, medication context or physician approval state. Therefore, Chapter~5 must place imaging analysis as one input to the clinical case rather than the method. The clinical domain depends on a dataset and validated model that can be used under the project constraints. Published capability does not establish completed integration or clinical effectiveness within Zeest.

\subsubsection{Critical Assessment}

BioViL-T extends the imaging discussion by learning from temporal structure in biomedical vision-language processing.~\cite{zeestB:biovilt} It directly informs Zeest because Zeest is concerned with more than a single isolated input; the integrated system must reason over patient information across time and modalities. However, a vision-language model still differs from the Zeest workflow. Zeest combines consultation notes, history retrieval, imaging outputs, laboratory information, medication context, evidence retrieval and critic review. A multimodal representation alone does not establish the audit trail or decision boundary required by Chapter~4. The review of imaging and vision-language research supports the need for careful integration. Imaging findings should retain their source and be displayed as specialist findings. They should not be silently merged into a generated conclusion without indicating how they relate to the encounter. This distinction motivates the text-only versus multimodal comparison in Chapter~5. The supported task and integration boundary must remain distinguishable during evaluation.

\subsubsection{Relevance to Zeest}

The imaging literature directly supports Zeest's plan to integrate an existing validated model instead of training a diagnostic model from scratch. It also supports the need for a multimodal methodology in which imaging findings are considered alongside notes, history, laboratory information and medication context. Zeest differs from isolated imaging systems by treating image analysis as a specialist contribution to a physician-reviewed case. The final draft remains subject to evidence retrieval, critic review and physician approval, so imaging output is not treated as an independent clinical decision. The connection also creates verification targets: inputs retain their sources, processing failures remain visible, and draft revisions preserve the evidence and critic findings presented to the physician. Component value must be compared under common case definitions and reference procedures. Resource selection does not change the requirement for explicit approval before clinical decision recording. Source-aware outputs preserve the link between component findings and physician review.

\subsection{Retrieval, Evidence Grounding and Medical Search}
\subsubsection{Research Overview}

Retrieval-augmented generation connects a generator with retrieved documents so that generated answers can use external knowledge.~\cite{zeestA:rag} This directly informs Zeest because a draft should be accompanied by evidence that the physician can inspect. General RAG, however, does not guarantee that retrieved passages are clinically appropriate, patient-specific or sufficient for a diagnosis. MedRAG examines retrieval-augmented generation in medical question answering and provides a closer medical context for evaluating retrieval and generation together.~\cite{zeestB:medrag} Its relevance lies in showing that retrieval choices can affect medical answer quality. Its limitation is that medical question answering still differs from a physician-facing encounter workflow with patient memory and approval actions. MedCPT contributes contrastive pre-training for biomedical retrieval using PubMed search logs, supporting the idea that medical retrieval benefits from domain-specific search representations.~\cite{zeestB:medcpt} Zeest can use these ideas only after selecting evidence sources and retrieval components. Published capability does not establish completed integration or effectiveness within Zeest.

\subsubsection{Critical Assessment}

The strength of RAG and medical retrieval work is that it gives a practical way to connect generated text with external sources. MedRAG and MedCPT are especially because they move retrieval closer to biomedical and medical question answering. The limitation is that retrieval does not automatically make an answer correct. Retrieved passages may be incomplete, irrelevant or poorly matched to the patient case. A generator may overstate what the retrieved evidence supports. These weaknesses are important for Zeest because a clinical draft must be reviewable by a physician. The system must keep evidence visible, cite sources and allow critic review of hiding retrieval inside the generated answer. A useful assessment must distinguish the capability demonstrated by the source from the additional workflow Zeest requires. Integration still needs patient ownership, source relationships and explicit review states. These responsibilities cannot be inferred from benchmark performance or the presence of a software tool.

\subsubsection{Relevance to Zeest}

The evidence literature supports two design principles for the system. First, evidence must remain visible and citable rather than disappearing into a generated answer. Second, retrieval quality is a variable to be evaluated, not an assumed property of the architecture. Chapter~4 reflects these principles through requirements for guideline retrieval, citation display and supporting passages. Chapter~5 extends them into an evidence-retrieval procedure in which patient context and external guideline search are separated before the draft is produced. The physician should be able to inspect whether a cited passage supports the draft. The Safety and Critic Agent should review unsupported or contradictory content before presentation. This approach is stricter than ordinary RAG demonstrations because the final workflow concerns a draft and an audit record. The literature motivates RAG use, while the project constraints define the safeguards needed for Zeest. Source-aware outputs preserve the link between component findings and physician review.

\subsection{Agentic Reasoning and Orchestration}
\subsubsection{Research Overview}


\subsubsection{Critical Assessment}

Agentic systems introduce risks that are especially important in a clinical report. More components can improve separation of responsibility, but they can create ambiguous handoffs, repeated context, failed tool calls or unsupported conclusions. Zeest's method must define what each stage receives and returns. The Patient Memory Agent retrieves history, the Clinical Case Agent organises the case, specialist agents produce typed findings, the Evidence and RAG Agent retrieves sources, the Clinical Reasoning Agent drafts the recommendation and the Safety and Critic Agent reviews it. The physician then approves, modifies or rejects the draft. This sequence is proposed, not executed. Its value will be examined through the ablation study rather than assumed in advance. ReAct and orchestration frameworks motivate the decomposition, while Zeest adds the patient-memory, evidence-inspection and physician-approval controls required for the medical setting. A useful assessment must distinguish the capability demonstrated by the source from the additional workflow Zeest requires.

\subsubsection{Relevance to Zeest}

This work supports Zeest's decision to divide responsibilities across specialised agents rather than using one prompt for the whole clinical task. The relationship is strongest in the idea that reasoning can be coordinated with tool use, retrieval and intermediate checks. Zeest extends that idea into a medical copilot workflow by adding patient memory, specialist clinical inputs, critic review and physician-controlled decision recording. The proposed research will compare this full agentic workflow against simpler variants so that the value of the decomposition can be assessed instead of assumed. \subsection{Clinical Datasets and Synthetic Cases}
\subsubsection{Research Overview}

MIMIC-IV provides de-identified electronic health record data and can support Zeest's longitudinal-memory objective because it contains patient and encounter-level clinical information.~\cite{zeestA:mimicIV} MIMIC-CXR provides a de-identified chest radiograph dataset with associated reports, thereby contributing to imaging and multimodal case preparation.~\cite{zeestB:mimiccxr} These datasets support the idea that de-identified clinical data can be used for research, but their existence does not by itself grant access, determine the disease domain or authorize every use. Synthea offers a method and software mechanism for generating synthetic patients.~\cite{zeestB:synthea} Its relevance is that synthetic cases can help develop workflows when real clinical data access is constrained. The limitation is that synthetic cases may not represent the full clinical complexity of real patients. Zeest's methodology distinguishes selected candidates, access status, preprocessing and final evaluation resources of treating all candidate datasets as already approved inputs. Resource permission and case suitability must be confirmed separately before incorporating candidates into comparative evaluation.

\subsubsection{Critical Assessment}

Across the dataset literature, the common lesson is that reproducible evaluation depends on case definition, permitted access, preprocessing, reference judgement and controlled comparisons. Zeest's planned evaluation therefore uses common case inputs across a single-LLM baseline, a memory-augmented variant and the full system. Any future results must come from those executed comparisons, not from claims imported from prior datasets or unrelated benchmark papers. A useful assessment must distinguish the capability demonstrated by the source from the additional clinical workflow Zeest requires. Integration still needs patient ownership, identifiable preserved source relationships and explicit physician review states. These responsibilities cannot be inferred from benchmark performance or the presence of an existing software tool. Dataset documentation describes available records rather than guaranteeing a usable evaluation case. Patient trajectories, supported modalities and source permissions require checking together. Reference judgements must be independent of generated answers, and configuration changes must remain identifiable when comparing system variants.

\subsubsection{Relevance to Zeest}

The dataset literature is related to Zeest because the project needs longitudinal cases, possible imaging inputs and safe development data. MIMIC-IV, MIMIC-CXR, CheXpert and Synthea provide candidate directions, but each has access, preprocessing and domain constraints. Zeest's proposed methodology therefore keeps a dataset and model register instead of claiming that every candidate resource is already selected. These resources will support the planned ablation study only after the final domain, permitted use and reference judgement process are confirmed. The connection also creates verification targets: inputs retain their sources, processing failures remain visible, and draft revisions preserve the evidence and critic findings presented to the responsible physician. Component value must be compared under common case definitions and reference procedures. Resource selection does not change the requirement for explicit approval before clinical decision recording. The selected cases must preserve patient and encounter context and source relationships while distinguishing missing modalities from completed findings.

\section{Conclusion}

The reviewed literature supports the need for Zeest but clarifies its boundaries. Clinical support systems and EMR platforms show the importance of diagnosis assistance, evidence access and patient records, yet they do not combine these functions into a longitudinal agentic workflow. Medical LLMs and biomedical language models provide useful reasoning and representation capabilities, but their benchmark performance does not remove the need for evidence, critic review and physician approval. Imaging and vision-language work supports the integration of validated models, while showing why imaging findings must remain tied to their source and task. RAG and medical retrieval research motivate cited evidence retrieval, but retrieval quality must be tested rather than assumed. Dataset work provides possible resources for evaluation, subject to access and domain constraints. The resulting gap is the controlled integration of patient memory, multimodal findings, evidence retrieval, critic review and physician decision logging. Comparisons establish the value of this integration.
